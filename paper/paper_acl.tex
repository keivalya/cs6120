\title{Measuring the Benchmark, Not the Model: Instruction Information Content
Confounds Word-Class Grounding Claims in Vision-Language-Action Models}

\author{Anonymous ACL Submission}

\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models map a natural-language instruction and a visual
observation to robot actions, but it remains unclear how much they rely on the
language rather than on visual priors and memorized task layouts. We study this with
a \emph{scene-fixed} causal evaluation that holds the initial simulator state
constant and perturbs only the instruction, on \texttt{LIBERO-Goal}, across three
architectures spanning a $16\times$ parameter range. Language is causally necessary
and scale-invariant: blanking the instruction or replacing it with length-matched
nonsense collapses success at every scale.

Our main contribution is negative. The widely reported reading of such results---that
VLAs bind object nouns strictly but treat action verbs as near-optional---does not
follow, because it is confounded by the information structure of the benchmark. In
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal} the noun set uniquely identifies all ten tasks
($H(\text{task}\mid\text{nouns})=0$ bits) while the verb leaves $1.55$ of $3.32$ bits
unresolved, so conditional on the nouns the verb is \emph{fully redundant} and
ignoring it is optimal rather than a grounding failure. Seven of the ten standard
verb substitutions preserve the noun set, so the probe cannot detect
verb-insensitivity at all; and the apparent ``collapse'' under object substitution is
compliance being mis-scored. A word-class \emph{removal} test confirms the account:
deleting the verb is indistinguishable from no edit (McNemar $p=0.34$) while masking
the nouns flips $148$ of $200$ matched pairs. Isolating the three tasks with true
antonym pairs reverses the usual reading---OpenVLA-7B loses $31.7$ points
($p<10^{-5}$) where the uninformative substitutions move it not at all
($p=0.52$)---so verb-insensitivity survives for exactly one of our three models. We
argue that instruction-level information content must be reported before grounding
claims are drawn from perturbation studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models extend large vision-language backbones with an
action head, promising robots that follow free-form natural-language instructions
\citep{brohan2022rt1,brohan2023rt2,driess2023palm,kim2025openvla,shukor2025smolvla}.
Their headline capability is \emph{language conditioning}: the same policy should
perform different manipulations depending on what it is told. Yet standard
benchmarks report a single aggregate success rate, which cannot distinguish a
policy that reads the instruction from one that has memorized a visually cued task
layout. Because instructions and scenes are correlated in most benchmarks---the
bowl-on-the-stove task looks different from the open-the-drawer task---a model can
score well while largely ignoring language.

We isolate the language channel with a \emph{scene-fixed} causal probe. The
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal} suite \citep{liu2023libero} shares one object inventory and
layout across its ten tasks, which differ only in the goal to be achieved, so a
policy cannot infer the task from the scene as it can where each task has its own
objects. Holding the initial simulator state strictly constant ($S_0=\text{const}$)
and perturbing \emph{only} the instruction string, we measure how behavior changes
under structural corruptions (blanking, length-matched nonsense), semantic
substitutions (swapping the object noun, the action verb, or the whole task), and
natural paraphrases. This turns a correlational question (``does success correlate
with language?'') into a causal one (``does changing the language change the
behavior, with everything else fixed?'').

Evaluating three architectures spanning $16\times$ in scale, we find that language is
causally necessary and scale-invariant: destroying the instruction destroys success at
every scale. This cuts against a rapidly forming consensus that VLAs are
``instruction-blind''
\citep{fei2025liberoplus,zhou2025liberopro,hou2026langgap,zhang2026flatness,grant2026features},
and we think both are right about their own settings---\texttt{Goal} is the LIBERO
suite that places heterogeneous goals in a \emph{shared} environment, so it is the one
where a policy cannot succeed by recognising the layout. Which regime a benchmark is
in is a property one can compute in advance, and mostly nobody does.

At first sight our results also reproduce the asymmetry that such probes usually
report: object swaps collapse success while verb swaps are tolerated, inviting the
conclusion that VLAs bind nouns strictly and treat verbs as near-optional. We set
out to explain that gap mechanistically and instead found that it is largely an
artifact of the measurement. Verbs in \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} carry no task
information once the nouns are known, so ignoring them is optimal rather than
deficient; most verb substitutions leave the task identity intact and so cannot
test verb grounding; and most of the object-swap ``collapse'' is the policy
correctly executing the instruction it was given, scored against the task it was
not. The residual effect is real but much narrower than the literature's framing
suggests.

The methodological point generalises beyond this suite: instruction perturbation
silently assumes that the perturbed word classes are informationally comparable, and
where they are not, the probe measures the benchmark rather than the model.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt \parskip0pt
    \item \textbf{Instruction information content as a confound.} Conclusions about
    which word classes a VLA grounds are not identifiable from perturbation studies
    without first measuring what each class contributes to task identity. On
    \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} nouns carry all $3.32$ bits and verbs none conditional on
    them, so verb-insensitivity is optimal and the standard verb-swap probe is
    uninformative by construction (Section~\ref{sec:info}).
    \item \textbf{A removal test that separates the two.} Because substitution
    mostly leaves the task unchanged, we remove classes outright: deleting the verb
    ($0$ bits) is free, masking the nouns ($3.32$ bits) is catastrophic---two edits
    of similar surface size, two orders of magnitude apart in effect
    (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item \textbf{Re-analysis of the ``asymmetric grounding gap''.} Both halves
    dissolve. Object-swap ``collapse'' is largely \emph{compliance}: where the swap
    yields another real task's instruction, all three models perform that task and
    never the original ($17/20$, $18/20$, $20/20$). Verb-swap ``tolerance'' is mostly
    measured on substitutions that leave task identity intact; splitting them
    reverses the conclusion for two of three models (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
    \item \textbf{A scene-fixed protocol with an automated invariant check} that
    fails loudly, motivated by finding the invariant silently broken by ordinary
    evaluation practice (Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed}), plus a kinematic read-out of
    \emph{when} behaviour diverges (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Vision-Language-Action models.} Instruction-conditioned manipulation
policies scale vision-language pretraining to robot control. RT-1 and RT-2
\citep{brohan2022rt1,brohan2023rt2} and PaLM-E \citep{driess2023palm} established
transformer policies that ingest language and pixels; OpenVLA \citep{kim2025openvla}
open-sourced a 7B Prismatic-backbone policy with discretized action tokens, and
SmolVLA \citep{shukor2025smolvla} showed a compact ($<1$B) alternative. We study
these families directly, adding OpenVLA-OFT (an L1-regression continuous-action
fine-tune), to probe whether language grounding changes with scale or action-head
design.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and robustness.} LIBERO \citep{liu2023libero} provides suites
of language-conditioned manipulation tasks; we use \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} precisely
because its shared initial scene lets us isolate the language channel.
LIBERO-Para \citep{kim2026liberopara} measures paraphrase robustness and introduces
the PRIDE metric we adopt in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}; LIBERO-Plus
\citep{fei2025liberoplus} perturbs seven visual and physical factors; and
\citet{pang2025openvla} show that OpenVLA succeeds mainly at object positions seen
in training, which is a warning that apparent competence on these suites can rest
on memorisation.

\paragraph{The instruction-blindness consensus.} A striking convergence has formed
around the claim that VLAs largely \emph{ignore} language. LIBERO-Plus finds models
insensitive to instruction variation while highly sensitive to viewpoint and initial
state. LIBERO-PRO \citep{zhou2025liberopro} reports outputs that ``remain unchanged
even when given corrupted instructions or even messy tokens'' and attributes this to
rote memorisation of action sequences and layouts; LangGap \citep{hou2026langgap}
reports the same for models scoring above $95\%$ on standard benchmarks and argues
that LIBERO's one-task-per-layout design never forces language use.
\citet{zhang2026flatness} name the failure mode ``instruction blindness'', while
\citet{zhan2026stable} describe a ``modality collapse'' towards a visual affordance
prior. Mechanistically, \citet{grant2026features} find across six models and $394$K
episodes that injecting baseline activations into null-prompt episodes recovers
near-identical behaviour.

\paragraph{Our position.} We find the opposite on \texttt{LIBERO-Goal}: deleting the
instruction destroys success at every scale. This does not contradict the work above,
and the reconciliation is the point of this paper. The LIBERO suites differ in how
much the scene reveals---\texttt{Spatial} varies positions of one object,
\texttt{Object} varies objects within similar layouts, and \texttt{Goal} places
\emph{heterogeneous goals in a shared environment}. Only in the last does the
observation fail to identify the task, so only there must a competent policy consult
the instruction; consistent with this, blank-instruction results in the literature are
strongly suite-dependent. Even there, redundancy has not disappeared---it has moved
down a level, from the instruction as a whole to the verb within it. This matters
because prior work increasingly proposes fixes: sharpness-aware finetuning
\citep{zhang2026flatness}, contrastive amplification of the language signal
\citep{zhan2026stable}, or suites built so language cannot be ignored
\citep{hou2026langgap,chen2026radar}. Each presumes we can correctly measure how much
a policy uses each part of an instruction, and we argue that measurement is not
identifiable from perturbation experiments alone.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
We conduct all evaluations on the \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} benchmarking suite in
MuJoCo, chosen because its tasks share an object inventory and scene layout, so
within a task the instruction is the only cue that disambiguates the goal. The
invariant our causal claims require is per-task: for a given (task, episode), the
initial state must be identical across all instruction conditions,
$S_0(I_{\text{orig}}) \equiv S_0(I_{\text{pert}})$. We verify it by hashing the
full simulator state (\texttt{qpos}, \texttt{qvel}) after reset and comparing
across conditions; Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed} reports what that check found. We
evaluate three checkpoints chosen to vary both scale and action-head design:
We evaluate \textbf{SmolVLA-0.45B} \citep{shukor2025smolvla}, a compact SmolVLM2
backbone with a \emph{flow-matching} action expert; \textbf{OpenVLA-7B}
\citep{kim2025openvla}, a Prismatic VLM over Llama-2 7B emitting \emph{discretized}
action tokens; and \textbf{OpenVLA-OFT-7.5B} \citep{kim2025oft}, the Optimized
Fine-Tuning recipe (parallel decoding, 8-step action chunks, a continuous action
representation and an L1-regression objective), which additionally consumes a wrist
camera and 8-dimensional proprioception. (OFT here is \emph{Optimized} Fine-Tuning,
not the unrelated orthogonal-fine-tuning method; the public LIBERO-Goal release we
evaluate is trained \emph{without} FiLM, so we make no FiLM claims.) The three span a
$16\times$ parameter range and all three major action parameterisations, which
matters for Section~\ref{sec:rq1}: a finding that holds across discretized,
continuous and flow-matching heads is unlikely to be an artifact of any one decoder.

Every condition is run under two seeds ($7$ and $42$) with a rollout horizon of
$T=300$ steps, and each reported cell pools all episodes for its (model, condition)
across both seeds.

numbers.

\subsection*{Metrics}
Let $\text{TSR}(c)$ be the Task Success Rate under condition $c$ and $\text{TSR}_0$
the \texttt{original} baseline. The \textbf{Causal Sensitivity Score}
$\text{CSS}(c) = (\text{TSR}_0 - \text{TSR}(c)) / \max(\text{TSR}_0,\epsilon)$ scores
the destructive conditions: $\text{CSS}\!\approx\!1$ means the instruction is causally
necessary, $\approx\!0$ that it is ignored. The \textbf{Original-Action Rate (OAR)}
is the fraction of semantic-swap episodes in which the policy still executes the
\emph{original} task despite the altered instruction; high OAR is direct evidence the
policy follows pixels over words. For paraphrases we use \textbf{PRIDE}
\citep{kim2026liberopara}, which weights each paraphrase's success by its distance
from the original (we reuse the reference implementation; see
Appendix~\ref{app:pride}). Finally, \textbf{conditional task entropy}
$H(\text{task}\mid W)$ is the residual uncertainty about which task an instruction
denotes given only its words of class $W$---a property of the benchmark rather than of
any model, used in Section~\ref{sec:info} to establish which perturbations can carry
evidence about grounding at all.

\section{Empirical Results \& Analysis}

\subsection{What the Instruction Actually Encodes}
\label{sec:info}
Perturbation studies infer which word classes a policy grounds by corrupting each
in turn and reading off the damage. This is only valid if the word classes carry
comparable information about the task. On \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} they do not, and
the gap is total rather than marginal.

We parse each of the ten instructions into its unordered noun set and its verb
set, and compute the residual uncertainty about task identity given each. With a
uniform prior over the ten tasks, $H(\text{task}) = \log_2 10 = 3.32$ bits:
% One line ran 76pt past the column; split across two.
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
H(\text{task}\mid\text{noun set}) &= 0.00\ \text{bits},\\
H(\text{task}\mid\text{verb set}) &= 1.55\ \text{bits}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
All ten noun sets are distinct, so the nouns alone determine the task. The verbs
do not: \texttt{put} is the verb of six of the ten tasks. Conditional on the
nouns, the verb therefore contributes \emph{exactly zero} bits.
Figure~\ref{fig:information} shows both the residual uncertainty and the partition
that produces it.

\textbf{(b)} Why: the noun sets partition the suite into ten singletons, while the

Two consequences follow, and both undercut the standard reading of these probes.
First, a policy that ignores the verb loses no task-relevant information on this
suite: verb-insensitivity is optimal, not a grounding deficit. Second, and more
damaging for the method, the verb-substitution probe cannot detect verb-insensitivity
even in principle---seven of its ten substitutions replace \texttt{put} with
\texttt{push}, leaving the noun set and hence the task unchanged. Only three tasks
(\texttt{open}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{close} twice,
\texttt{turn on}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{turn off} once) substitute a true antonym
where the nouns cannot disambiguate the goal; we report those separately throughout
and treat them as the only valid verb probes the suite affords. This is a property of
the benchmark, not of any model, computed from the task definitions with no rollouts,
and we suggest it be reported as a matter of course before word-class grounding claims
are drawn from perturbation experiments.

\subsection{The Decisive Test: Removal, Not Substitution}
\label{sec:ablation}
The argument above makes a sharp prediction, and it is falsifiable. Instead of
\emph{substituting} within a word class---which, as just shown, mostly fails to
change the task---\emph{remove} a class outright and measure the cost. Dropping the
verb (``the middle drawer of the cabinet'') removes $0$ bits of task information and
should cost close to nothing. Masking the nouns (``open the thing of the thing'')
removes all $3.32$ bits and should be catastrophic. Had verb removal instead
collapsed performance, the redundancy account would be wrong: the information
analysis would be describing the task distribution while missing what the policy
actually consumes.

The prediction holds, with an unusually clean separation
(Table~\ref{tab:rq_ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:removal}). Deleting the verb leaves
OpenVLA-OFT's success rate statistically unchanged on the same initial scenes: a point
estimate of $\oftVerbDrop$ points with a $95\%$ interval of $[-5.0, +1.0]$ that
contains zero, and a McNemar $p$ of $0.34$. Of $200$ matched pairs only $10$ changed
outcome at all, $7$ one way and $3$ the other, which is what a coin looks like.
Masking the nouns costs $\oftNounMask$ points, flips $148$ pairs, and never once flips
a failure into a success. Because every perturbed episode starts from its baseline's
simulator state these are matched pairs, so the discordant counts are the evidence and
the concordant majority correctly drops out. Two edits of comparable surface
magnitude differ by two orders of magnitude in effect; what separates them is not how
much text was deleted but how much task information it carried.

One detail is worth noting: \texttt{nouns\_masked} remains clearly \emph{above}
\texttt{blank}, even though on our accounting both destroy all $3.32$ bits. The masked
instruction retains the verb and the syntactic frame, so the residual gap estimates
what a policy extracts from instruction \emph{structure} once reference is gone---more
than nothing, and much less than the nouns provide.

\subsection{The Scene-Fixed Invariant Is Easy to Break Silently}
\label{sec:scenefixed}
Our protocol requires that all conditions for a (task, episode) start from an
identical simulator state. Auditing this with the state hash, we found the invariant
violated across most of our initial data, for a reason we believe generalises to
other LIBERO-based evaluations: the initial state is \emph{not} a pure function of
(task, episode), because each \texttt{reset} advances the generator seeded once at
construction, so the scene an episode receives depends on how many resets its process
has already served. Any condition completed by a \emph{second} process---after a
wall-clock kill, or simply because conditions were run in separate jobs---silently
lands on different scenes while the output files look ordered, complete and plausible
(Appendix~\ref{app:scenefixed}).

This matters beyond bookkeeping: recomputing our paraphrase deltas against a
scene-matched rather than a pooled baseline moves them by up to $12$ percentage
points, comparable to the effect sizes such studies report. We therefore (i) treat
the task as the atomic unit of evaluation, so every condition for a task is produced
by one process or regenerated wholesale, and (ii) make the invariant check a hard
failure in aggregation rather than a logged diagnostic. Both are released with the
code.

The destructive conditions behave as expected: \texttt{blank} and \texttt{nonsense}
collapse success, establishing that the instruction is causally load-bearing.
SmolVLA falls to exactly zero under both ($\text{CSS}=1.00$); OpenVLA-OFT retains a
small residue, $\oftBlank\%$ and $\oftNonsense\%$ against a $\oftBaseline\%$ baseline
($\text{CSS}=\oftCssBlank$ and $\oftCssNonsense$), which we return to in the
Discussion. Nothing here
is a graded contrast, so the conclusion does not rest on the exact residue.
The effect is flat in scale: CSS is $1.00$, $1.00$ and $\oftCssBlank$ across a
$16\times$ parameter range, so sensitivity to losing the instruction tracks neither
model size nor baseline competence.
The two semantic-swap conditions are the ones that have been over-read, and we take
them in turn.

\paragraph{What the scene-fixed audit lets us claim.} A model whose grid fails the
check (Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed}) is left blank rather than printed with a
footnote. This is not hypothetical: every number reported here for OpenVLA-7B is from
its \emph{second} grid, the first having failed on $20$ of $80$ comparable episodes.
All three grids now pass at $200$ comparable episodes with zero mismatches. A scene
confound can inflate or deflate a partial difference but cannot manufacture a floor,
and \texttt{wrong\_task} sits at exactly $0\%$ for every model and episode, so the
CSS$\,\approx\!1$ result is the robust part and the intermediate rates the fragile
one. We lean only on the former.

\paragraph{Object swaps measure compliance, not collapse.}
Read naively, \texttt{wrong\_object} triggers a near-total collapse in TSR. But TSR
scores the policy against the \emph{original} task, so a policy that correctly follows
the altered instruction is recorded as a failure. The condition also mixes cases: the
perturbed string may name another real task, describe a novel but coherent goal, or be
degenerate (``put the bowl in the bowl''), where failure says nothing about grounding.
The first is decisive. For one task the object swap yields, verbatim, another task's
instruction, and every model performs \emph{that} task and never the original: SmolVLA
$17/20$, OpenVLA $18/20$ and OpenVLA-OFT $20/20$, with $0$ executions of the original
by any model. This is precise instruction-following, scored as total failure. We
therefore report OAR against the \emph{named} task alongside TSR, and caution that
headline ``collapse'' numbers under object substitution should not be read as loss of
grounding.

\paragraph{Verb swaps mostly do not test verbs.}
Seven of the ten verb substitutions preserve the noun set and therefore the task
identity (Section~\ref{sec:info}), so surviving them is expected of any competent
policy and says nothing about verb grounding. Splitting the condition into the three
genuine antonym pairs and the seven near-synonyms separates the informative cases
from the rest (Table~\ref{tab:rq2_wordclass}).

The split changes the conclusion, and OpenVLA-7B shows why in the sharpest form
(Figure~\ref{fig:verbsplit}).
Read whole, its \texttt{wrong\_action} condition costs $12$ points---a mild effect,
the kind routinely reported as evidence that verbs are near-optional. Split, that
average dissolves into two opposite findings: the three antonym substitutions cost
it $31.7$ points ($p<10^{-5}$, $19$ of $60$ pairs flipped to failure and none the
other way), while the seven near-synonyms cost $3.6$ and are indistinguishable from
no edit at all ($p=0.52$). OpenVLA reads the verb, decisively, and the suite-wide
probe hides it by averaging one real effect against seven non-events.

SmolVLA behaves the same way, less starkly: $21.7$ points on antonyms ($p=0.002$)
against $10.7$ on near-synonyms ($p=0.02$). OpenVLA-OFT is the exception, losing
$5.0$ points on antonyms and $2.1$ on near-synonyms with \emph{neither}
distinguishable from zero ($p=0.45$, $p=0.51$); told to ``turn off the stove'' it
turns the stove on, completing the \emph{original} task in $55$ of $60$ episodes. The
verb-insensitivity the literature attributes to VLAs in general is, on this suite, a
property of one of the three models we tested---and the probe usually used to
establish it could not have detected it in the other two.

The two 7B models also make a distinction visible that deserves care.
\emph{Removing} the verb is free for both---OpenVLA gains $4.0$ points, OpenVLA-OFT
loses $2.0$, neither detectable (Table~\ref{tab:rq_ablation})---while
\emph{contradicting} it costs OpenVLA $31.7$. Redundant is not the same as ignored: a
word can carry no task information, so that deleting it changes nothing, and still be
read closely enough that asserting its opposite derails the policy. Only comparing
removal against substitution separates those two states.

This split remains the most underpowered comparison in the paper: the suite affords
only three antonym pairs, so $n$ grows with episodes but never with tasks. An earlier
draft, built when each cell held two episodes, put OpenVLA-OFT's antonym gap at
exactly zero on $n=12$; at $n=60$ the estimate is $5.0$ points, and the interval
$[-13.3, +3.3]$ covers both. The lesson runs against the point estimates, not for
them.

\subsection{Paraphrase Robustness}
\label{sec:rq2}
Corrupting an instruction asks whether language is used at all. Paraphrasing it asks
a harder question: whether the policy has bound to \emph{meaning} or to surface form.
We rewrite each instruction along three axes---the object expression, the action
expression, and both at once---and score the results with PRIDE
\citep{kim2026liberopara}, which weights each paraphrase's success by how far it
departs from the original (Table~\ref{tab:rq2_para}).

The spread across models is large and orders with scale: SmolVLA degrades severely
($4.72\%$ overall TSR, PRIDE $2.7$), OpenVLA sits in between ($44.22\%$, PRIDE
$33.3$), and OpenVLA-OFT holds $74.22\%$ (PRIDE $65.8$). All three order the axes the same way---object paraphrases hurt least, compositional
most---as expected if the noun phrase is what the policy keys on. We do not attribute
OpenVLA-OFT's robustness to FiLM: the checkpoint we evaluate is the plain OFT release
(\texttt{use\_film=false}), so that comparison is untested.

Two measurement caveats bound how these numbers should be read, both consequences of
Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed}. First, $\Delta$TSR compares a paraphrase pool against
the \texttt{original} pool, and the two were produced by different processes, so
scene overlap has to be checked rather than assumed; restricting the baseline to the
matched subset shifts each delta by roughly ten percentage points. The \emph{ordering}
across axes (object $<$ action $<$ compositional) is identical under either baseline,
so we treat the ordering as the result and the magnitudes as provisional, and ship
both in the released CSV. Second, episode counts overstate the independent variation
behind these estimates: SmolVLA's $2963$ compositional paraphrase episodes span only
two distinct initial scenes per task, because the paraphrase runs predate our
initial-state cycling fix. We therefore report distinct scenes alongside $n$, and
avoid confidence intervals computed as though episodes were independent draws.

\subsection{When the Trajectories Part}
\label{sec:rq3}
Because perturbed and canonical rollouts share $S_0$, their end-effector traces are
comparable step by step. Defining $t_{\text{div}}$ as the first step at which they
differ by more than $5$\,cm, the kinematics match the causal results: with no usable
instruction (\texttt{blank}, \texttt{nonsense}) the policy departs within the first
step or two and never begins the right motion, whereas under \texttt{wrong\_action}
it begins the right motion and holds it for six to eleven steps before diverging---as
expected when the noun set has already determined where to go and only the manner of
acting is in question. We report this as a qualitative ordering rather than a precise
timing, since pair counts per cell are small and $t_{\text{div}}$ depends on the
$5$\,cm threshold. Appendix~\ref{app:kinematics} gives the curves and per-cell values.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
We presented a scene-fixed causal and paraphrase evaluation of three
Vision-Language-Action models spanning $16\times$ in scale. Three things are worth
separating in what we found.

\paragraph{Language is load-bearing, and scale does not change that.} Destroying the
instruction destroys success for all three architectures, across a $16\times$ range
and across discretized, continuous and flow-matching action heads. Nothing suggests a
bigger backbone learns to do without the instruction. This is the one headline that
came through the re-analysis untouched.

\paragraph{The asymmetry we set out to explain was mostly ours to begin with.} We
began intending to give a mechanistic account of why VLAs bind nouns more tightly
than verbs, and found that on this suite there is little to explain: the verb carries
no task information once the nouns are known; seven of ten verb probes cannot detect
verb-blindness even in principle; and most of the object-swap ``collapse'' is a policy
doing what it was told, scored against a task it was not asked to do. The numbers are
not in dispute---our TSRs reproduce the pattern others report---only what may be
concluded from them. What survives is narrower and better supported: on the only
three tasks where a verb swap changes the goal, OpenVLA-7B loses $31.7$ points
($p<10^{-5}$) and SmolVLA $21.7$ ($p=0.002$), while OpenVLA-OFT shows no detectable
response ($p=0.45$). Verb-insensitivity is a property of one of these models, not of
the class, and the suite-wide probe conventionally used to establish it reports a
mild $-12.0$ points for the model it least describes.

\paragraph{Extra observation streams and residual shortcutting.} OpenVLA-OFT is the
only model here with a wrist camera and proprioception, and the only one retaining
measurable success when the instruction is unusable ($\oftBlank\%$ under
\texttt{blank}, against exactly zero for the single-camera SmolVLA). Richer
non-linguistic input enabling a visual shortcut is the most plausible reading, but
with one model in that configuration we cannot separate the observation streams from
the action head or the fine-tuning recipe, and do not claim to. It is not
attributable to FiLM, which this checkpoint does not use.

\paragraph{What follows for the field.} Instruction-perturbation studies inherit the
information structure of their benchmark, and that structure has to be measured before
behavioural results can be read as evidence about grounding. This bears directly on
the mitigations now being proposed \citep{zhan2026stable,zhang2026flatness}. If a
benchmark's verbs are redundant given its nouns, a method that increases verb
sensitivity is scored on a probe that cannot distinguish better grounding from a
policy pushed away from the optimal one---so we do not dispute the reported gains, we
question what they are measured against. What the field needs is suites built with
verb-contrastive minimal pairs, of which \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} affords exactly three.
Until they exist, the honest move is to publish the instruction set's information
content alongside the success rates.

\section*{Limitations}
Our study is deliberately narrow to keep the causal claim clean, and this bounds
its scope. (i) \textbf{Single suite}: the causal isolation relies on
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal}'s shared initial scene and does not transfer to
scene-confounded suites; we therefore do not claim the asymmetry holds under
visual variation. (ii) \textbf{Simulation only}: all results are in MuJoCo/LIBERO
with no real-robot evaluation. (iii) \textbf{Sample size}: several cells rest on a
modest number of episodes, so we treat the qualitative pattern rather than any point
estimate as the claim, and report $n$ and a confidence interval for every cell so a
reader can see which comparisons are underpowered. The antonym contrast is the
narrowest of them: $60$ episodes per model, but only ever three tasks, so episodes
buy precision and never coverage---which is why we read it as a claim about one model
rather than a trend. We note that going from two episodes per cell to ten moved
OpenVLA-OFT's antonym gap from $0.0$ to $5.0$ points, so we do not treat the
remaining point estimates as settled either. (iv) \textbf{Scope of the information argument}: $H(\text{task}\mid W)$ is
computed over this suite's ten instructions under a uniform task prior. It shows
that verbs are redundant \emph{here}; it does not show that VLAs would ignore
verbs on a suite where verbs are contrastive, and our three antonym pairs are too
few to settle that. The uniform prior is itself an assumption: a policy trained on a
skewed task distribution faces a different effective entropy than the one we compute
from the task definitions.
(v) \textbf{Coverage of the removal test}: the removal conditions were run on both
7B models and replicate ($n=200$ each; verb deletion undetectable at $p=0.36$ and
$p=0.34$, noun masking at $p<10^{-10}$ for both). They were not run on SmolVLA,
whose $\sim\!161$\,s per episode made two extra conditions cost roughly $18$ GPU-hours
we chose to spend on the causal grid instead; that omission is a budget decision, not
a methodological one, and SmolVLA's \texttt{blank} condition is consistent with the
same account. (vi) \textbf{No mechanistic account}: we deliberately make no
attention-level claim. An earlier draft reported a layer-wise noun-vs-verb
cross-attention ratio; we found the underlying extraction had compared one model
against a relabelled copy of another and had fed the OpenVLA-family models a blank
image, so we removed the analysis rather than report it, and we leave a corrected
measurement to future work rather than claim one here. Readers wanting a mechanistic picture should see
\citet{grant2026features}, who use activation injection, sparse autoencoders and
linear probes across six models; our contribution is behavioural and
information-theoretic, and is compatible with their finding that the visual pathway
dominates on suites where the scene identifies the task. (vii) \textbf{Models}: we
evaluate three open checkpoints but a single benchmark family, and the coverage
actually reported is whatever passes the scene-fixed check---see the draft-status
note in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The $16\times$ scale range spanned by the models we
report on is what carries the scale-invariance claim; it does not depend on the
intermediate checkpoint.

\section*{Ethics and Reproducibility}
This work uses only simulated manipulation tasks and public model checkpoints; it
involves no human subjects or personal data. The primary societal consideration is
safety: a policy that silently ignores the action verb could execute an unintended
manipulation while appearing to follow instructions, which our diagnostics are
designed to expose. For reproducibility we release per-run provenance (model,
checkpoint hash, code revision, seed and wall-clock in a run manifest), pinned
environment locks for each backbone, and the scene-fixed invariance audit. Every
table in this paper is generated from the result files by a script rather than
transcribed by hand; we adopted that rule after finding that hand-copied numbers
were how an error survived several drafts of this work.

\section{How the Scene-Fixed Invariant Breaks}
\label{app:scenefixed}
The LIBERO environment is seeded once at construction, and each \texttt{reset}
advances that generator, which perturbs object poses even though the stored initial
state pins \texttt{qpos} and \texttt{qvel}. A condition therefore inherits whatever
generator position its process has reached. In our data the clearest case is a single
condition split across a job boundary: episode~9 sits on a different scene from
episodes~0--8 of the same condition. The remedy we adopt is to treat the task as the
atomic unit of evaluation, so every condition for a task is produced by one process or
regenerated wholesale, and to make the invariant check a hard failure in aggregation.

\section{PRIDE}
\label{app:pride}
PRIDE \citep{kim2026liberopara} weights each paraphrase's success by how far it
departs from the original instruction:
$\text{PD}_i = 1-(\alpha \text{SK}_i + (1-\alpha) \text{ST}_i)$ and
$\text{PRIDE} = 100 \sum_i s_i \text{PD}_i / \sum_i \text{PD}_i$, where $s_i$ is
success and $\text{SK}, \text{ST}$ are keyword and structural similarities.

\section{Kinematic Divergence}
\label{app:kinematics}

following the original plan. Because our design gives the perturbed and canonical
rollouts the same $S_0$, we can compare their end-effector traces step by step and
ask when they separate. We define $t_{\text{div}}$ as the first step at which the
two traces differ by more than $5$\,cm (Table~\ref{tab:rq3_divergence},
Appendix~\ref{app:full}).
