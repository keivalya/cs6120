\title{Measuring the Benchmark, Not the Model: Instruction Information Content
Confounds Word-Class Grounding Claims in Vision-Language-Action Models}

\author{Anonymous ACL Submission}

\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models map a natural-language instruction and a
visual observation to robot actions, but it remains unclear how much they rely on
the language rather than on visual priors and memorized task layouts. We study
this with a \emph{scene-fixed} causal evaluation that holds the initial simulator
state constant and perturbs only the instruction, on the \texttt{LIBERO-Goal}
suite, across three architectures spanning a $16\times$ parameter range
(SmolVLA-0.45B, OpenVLA-7B, OpenVLA-OFT-7.5B). Language is causally necessary and
scale-invariant: blanking the instruction or replacing it with length-matched
nonsense collapses task success at every scale (Causal Sensitivity Score $0.85$
to $1.00$).

Our main contribution is negative and, we argue, more useful. A widely reported
reading of results like ours is that VLAs bind object nouns strictly but treat
action verbs as near-optional---an ``asymmetric grounding gap''. We show this
conclusion does not follow, because it is confounded by the information structure
of the benchmark itself. In \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} the unordered set of nouns in an
instruction \emph{uniquely} identifies all ten tasks ($H(\text{task}\mid
\text{nouns})=0$ bits), while the verb leaves $1.55$ of the $3.32$ bits unresolved
---its commonest form, \texttt{put}, covers six of the ten tasks by itself.
Conditional on the nouns the
verb is therefore \emph{fully redundant}, and ignoring it is the
information-theoretically optimal policy rather than a grounding failure.
Correspondingly, the standard verb-substitution probe cannot detect
verb-insensitivity at all: seven of its ten substitutions preserve the noun set
and hence the task identity. We further show that the apparent ``collapse'' under
object substitution is compliance being mis-scored: when the perturbation happens
to name a \emph{different real task}, the models perform that task and never
the original.

A word-class \emph{removal} test confirms the account directly: deleting the verb
outright costs OpenVLA-OFT $\oftVerbDrop$ points against a scene-matched baseline,
while masking the nouns costs it $\oftNounMask$---two edits of similar surface
magnitude separated by the task information they destroy. Because every perturbed
rollout starts from its baseline's exact initial state, these are matched pairs and
we test them as such: verb deletion is indistinguishable from no edit at all
(McNemar $p=0.34$), while noun masking flips $148$ of $200$ pairs. Isolating the
cases that do carry a contrast---three tasks with true antonym verb pairs---reverses
the usual reading: OpenVLA-7B loses $31.7$ points there ($p<10^{-5}$) while the seven
uninformative substitutions move it not at all ($p=0.52$), so the standard probe hides
a decisive verb effect by averaging it against non-events. Verb-insensitivity survives
for exactly one of our three models, OpenVLA-OFT, which is unmoved even by an antonym
($p=0.45$). We release the scene-fixed protocol, an automated
invariant check that fails loudly, and the word-class probes, and argue that
instruction-level information content must be reported before grounding claims can be
drawn from perturbation studies---including before crediting a mitigation with having
improved grounding.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Vision-Language-Action (VLA) models extend large vision-language backbones with an
action head, promising robots that follow free-form natural-language instructions
\citep{brohan2022rt1,brohan2023rt2,driess2023palm,kim2025openvla,shukor2025smolvla}.
Their headline capability is \emph{language conditioning}: the same policy should
perform different manipulations depending on what it is told. Yet standard
benchmarks report a single aggregate success rate, which cannot distinguish a
policy that reads the instruction from one that has memorized a visually cued task
layout. Because instructions and scenes are correlated in most benchmarks---the
bowl-on-the-stove task looks different from the open-the-drawer task---a model can
score well while largely ignoring language.

We isolate the language channel with a \emph{scene-fixed} causal probe. The
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal} suite \citep{liu2023libero} shares one object inventory and
layout across its ten tasks, which differ only in the goal to be achieved, so a
policy cannot infer the task from the scene as it can where each task has its own
objects. Holding the initial simulator state strictly constant ($S_0=\text{const}$)
and perturbing \emph{only} the instruction string, we measure how behavior changes
under structural corruptions (blanking, length-matched nonsense), semantic
substitutions (swapping the object noun, the action verb, or the whole task), and
natural paraphrases. This turns a correlational question (``does success correlate
with language?'') into a causal one (``does changing the language change the
behavior, with everything else fixed?'').

Evaluating three architectures spanning $16\times$ in scale, we find that language is
causally necessary and scale-invariant: destroying the instruction destroys success at
every scale. This cuts against a rapidly forming consensus that VLAs are
``instruction-blind''
\citep{fei2025liberoplus,zhou2025liberopro,hou2026langgap,zhang2026flatness,grant2026features},
and we think both are right about their own settings. The LIBERO suites differ in how
much the scene gives away: \texttt{Goal} is the one that places heterogeneous goals in
a \emph{shared} environment, so it is the one where a policy cannot succeed by
recognising the layout. Instruction-blindness is what you measure where the scene
identifies the task; language dependence is what you measure where it does not. Which
regime a benchmark is in is a property one can compute in advance, and mostly nobody
does.

At first sight our results also reproduce the asymmetry that such probes usually
report: object swaps collapse success while verb swaps are tolerated, inviting the
conclusion that VLAs bind nouns strictly and treat verbs as near-optional. We set
out to explain that gap mechanistically and instead found that it is largely an
artifact of the measurement. Verbs in \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} carry no task
information once the nouns are known, so ignoring them is optimal rather than
deficient; most verb substitutions leave the task identity intact and so cannot
test verb grounding; and most of the object-swap ``collapse'' is the policy
correctly executing the instruction it was given, scored against the task it was
not. The residual effect is real but much narrower than the literature's framing
suggests.

We think the methodological point generalises beyond this suite. Instruction
perturbation is becoming a standard tool for probing grounding, and it silently
assumes that the perturbed word classes are informationally comparable. Where they
are not, the probe measures the benchmark rather than the model. We therefore
report the information content of the instruction set as a precondition for
interpreting the behavioural results, and release the tooling to do so.

\subsection*{Contributions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Instruction information content as a confound}: We show that
    conclusions about \emph{which word classes a VLA grounds} are not identifiable
    from perturbation studies without first measuring what each word class
    contributes to task identity. On \texttt{LIBERO-Goal}, nouns carry all $3.32$
    bits and verbs carry none conditional on them, so verb-insensitivity is
    optimal behaviour and the standard verb-swap probe is uninformative by
    construction (Section~\ref{sec:info}).
    \item \textbf{A removal test that separates the two}: substituting within a word
    class mostly leaves the task unchanged, so we remove classes outright. Deleting
    the verb ($0$ bits) is free; masking the nouns ($3.32$ bits) is catastrophic.
    Two edits of similar surface size, two orders of magnitude apart in effect
    (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).
    \item \textbf{Re-analysis of the ``Asymmetric Grounding Gap''}: We show both
    halves of the reported asymmetry dissolve under this analysis. Object-swap
    ``collapse'' is largely \emph{compliance}: when the swap yields another real
    task's instruction, all three models execute that task and never the original
    ($17/20$, $18/20$ and $20/20$ episodes).
    Verb-swap ``tolerance'' is mostly measured on substitutions that leave task
    identity intact. Splitting them reverses the conclusion for two of three
    models: OpenVLA-7B loses $31.7$ pp to a contrastive verb ($p<10^{-5}$) and
    nothing at all to an uninformative one ($p=0.52$). Only OpenVLA-OFT is
    genuinely unmoved ($-5.0$ pp, $p=0.45$) (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).
    \item \textbf{Scene-Fixed Protocol and an Automated Invariant Check}: We
    present the diagnostic paradigm holding initial simulator states constant
    ($S_0 = \text{const}$) across 3 VLA architectures, and---because we found the
    invariant is silently broken by ordinary evaluation practice
    (Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed})---an automated check that fails loudly rather
    than reporting a confounded contrast.
    \item \textbf{A kinematic read-out of when behaviour diverges}: because
    perturbed and canonical rollouts start from a byte-identical state, their
    end-effector traces are directly comparable step by step. Destructive
    corruptions separate almost immediately, whereas verb substitutions track the
    canonical trajectory for a while before diverging---a behavioural signature
    that success rates alone hide (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
\paragraph{Vision-Language-Action models.} Instruction-conditioned manipulation
policies scale vision-language pretraining to robot control. RT-1 and RT-2
\citep{brohan2022rt1,brohan2023rt2} and PaLM-E \citep{driess2023palm} established
transformer policies that ingest language and pixels; OpenVLA \citep{kim2025openvla}
open-sourced a 7B Prismatic-backbone policy with discretized action tokens, and
SmolVLA \citep{shukor2025smolvla} showed a compact ($<1$B) alternative. We study
these families directly, adding OpenVLA-OFT (an L1-regression continuous-action
fine-tune), to probe whether language grounding changes with scale or action-head
design.

\paragraph{Benchmarks and robustness.} LIBERO \citep{liu2023libero} provides suites
of language-conditioned manipulation tasks; we use \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} precisely
because its shared initial scene lets us isolate the language channel.
LIBERO-Para \citep{kim2026liberopara} measures paraphrase robustness and introduces
the PRIDE metric we adopt in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}; LIBERO-Plus
\citep{fei2025liberoplus} perturbs seven visual and physical factors; and
\citet{pang2025openvla} show that OpenVLA succeeds mainly at object positions seen
in training, which is a warning that apparent competence on these suites can rest
on memorisation.

\paragraph{The instruction-blindness consensus.} A striking convergence has formed
around the claim that VLAs largely \emph{ignore} language. LIBERO-Plus finds models
insensitive to instruction variation while highly sensitive to viewpoint and initial
state. LIBERO-PRO \citep{zhou2025liberopro} reports that outputs ``remain unchanged
even when given corrupted instructions or even messy tokens'', with accuracy falling
from over $90\%$ to $0.0\%$ once perturbations are generalised, and attributes this
to rote memorisation of action sequences and layouts. LangGap
\citep{hou2026langgap} reports the same for models scoring above $95\%$ on standard
benchmarks and argues that LIBERO's one-task-per-layout design never forces language
use. \citet{zhang2026flatness} name the failure mode ``instruction blindness'' and
trace it to sharp loss landscapes during finetuning on limited robot data;
\citet{zhan2026stable} describe a ``modality collapse'' in which dense visual
features dominate the gradient and the policy degenerates towards a visual
affordance prior. Mechanistically, \citet{grant2026features} find across six models
and $394$K episodes that injecting baseline activations into null-prompt episodes
recovers near-identical behaviour, i.e.\ the visual pathway alone largely determines
the action.

\paragraph{Why our result is not a counterexample to it.} We find the opposite on
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal}: deleting the instruction destroys success at every scale. We
do not think this contradicts the work above, and the reconciliation is the point of
this paper. The LIBERO suites differ precisely in how much the scene reveals about
the task---\texttt{Spatial} varies positions of the same object,
\texttt{Object} varies objects within similar layouts, and \texttt{Goal} places
\emph{heterogeneous goals in a shared environment}. Only in the last does the visual
observation fail to identify the task, so only there must a competent policy consult
the instruction. Consistent with this, blank-instruction results in the literature
are strongly suite-dependent rather than uniform. What our suite buys is not a
different species of model but a setting where the language channel is
load-bearing---and the finding is that even there, redundancy has not disappeared,
it has merely moved down a level, from the instruction as a whole to the verb within
it.

\paragraph{Our position.} Prior work establishes \emph{that} VLAs are fragile to
input variation, and increasingly proposes fixes: sharpness-aware finetuning
\citep{zhang2026flatness}, paraphrase-marginalised training and contrastive
amplification of the language signal \citep{zhan2026stable}, or new suites built so
that language cannot be ignored \citep{hou2026langgap,chen2026radar}. Each presumes
we can correctly measure how much a policy uses each part of an instruction. We
argue that this measurement is not identifiable from perturbation experiments alone,
because the perturbed word classes are generally not informationally comparable, and
that reporting the instruction set's information content is a cheap precondition for
interpreting any of these results---including for deciding whether a proposed fix
has improved grounding or merely improved a metric that was never measuring
grounding.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
We conduct all evaluations on the \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} benchmarking suite in
MuJoCo, chosen because its tasks share an object inventory and scene layout, so
within a task the instruction is the only cue that disambiguates the goal. The
invariant our causal claims require is per-task: for a given (task, episode), the
initial state must be identical across all instruction conditions,
$S_0(I_{\text{orig}}) \equiv S_0(I_{\text{pert}})$. We verify it by hashing the
full simulator state (\texttt{qpos}, \texttt{qvel}) after reset and comparing
across conditions; Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed} reports what that check found. We
evaluate three checkpoints chosen to vary both scale and action-head design:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{SmolVLA-0.45B} \citep{shukor2025smolvla}: a compact SmolVLM2
  backbone (SigLIP vision encoder, SmolLM2 language model) with a
  \emph{flow-matching} action expert, at $256\times256$ observations.
\item \textbf{OpenVLA-7B} \citep{kim2025openvla}: a Prismatic VLM backbone over
  Llama-2 7B that emits \emph{discretized} action tokens, at $224\times224$.
\item \textbf{OpenVLA-OFT-7.5B} \citep{kim2025oft}: the Optimized Fine-Tuning
  recipe---parallel decoding, an 8-step action chunk, a continuous action
  representation and an L1-regression objective---which additionally consumes a
  wrist camera and 8-dimensional proprioception. (OFT here is
  \emph{Optimized} Fine-Tuning, not the unrelated orthogonal-fine-tuning method.)
  The public LIBERO-Goal release we evaluate is trained \emph{without} FiLM, so we
  make no FiLM claims.
\end{itemize}
The three therefore span a $16\times$ parameter range and all three major action
parameterisations in current VLAs, which matters for Section~\ref{sec:rq1}: a
finding that holds across discretized, continuous and flow-matching heads is
unlikely to be an artifact of any one decoder.

Every condition is run under two seeds ($7$ and $42$) with a rollout horizon of
$T=300$ steps, and each reported cell pools all episodes for its (model, condition)
across both seeds.

deliberately, because it is applied identically to every condition, and every claim we
make is a within-model contrast across conditions at a fixed horizon. A truncation
that removes the same tail from the baseline and the perturbed cells alike cannot
create the differences we report, though it does mean our absolute rates should not be
compared with published leaderboard numbers.

\subsection*{Metrics}
Let $\text{TSR}(c)$ be the Task Success Rate (fraction of episodes reaching the
goal predicate) under condition $c$, and $\text{TSR}_0$ the baseline
(\texttt{original} instruction). We use four aggregate diagnostics, plus the
trajectory-level measure $t_{\text{div}}$ defined in Section~\ref{sec:rq3}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Causal Sensitivity Score (CSS)}, for the destructive conditions
  \texttt{blank}/\texttt{nonsense}:
  $\text{CSS}(c) = \big(\text{TSR}_0 - \text{TSR}(c)\big) / \max(\text{TSR}_0,\epsilon)$.
  $\text{CSS}\!\approx\!1$ means the instruction is causally necessary;
  $\text{CSS}\!\approx\!0$ means it is ignored.
\item \textbf{Original-Action Rate (OAR)}, for the semantic-swap conditions
  (\texttt{wrong\_object}/\texttt{wrong\_action}/\texttt{wrong\_task}): the fraction
  of episodes in which the policy still executes the \emph{original} task despite
  the altered instruction, scored against the simulator goal predicates. High OAR
  is direct evidence the policy follows pixels over words.
\item \textbf{PRIDE} \citep{kim2026liberopara}: a paraphrase-robustness score that
  weights each paraphrase's success by its distance from the original instruction,
  $\text{PD}_i = 1-\big(\alpha\, \text{SK}_i + (1-\alpha)\, \text{ST}_i\big)$ and
  $\text{PRIDE} = 100\cdot \sum_i s_i\,\text{PD}_i / \sum_i \text{PD}_i$, where
  $s_i$ is success and $\text{SK},\text{ST}$ are keyword/structural similarities;
  we reuse the reference implementation.
\item \textbf{Conditional task entropy} $H(\text{task}\mid W)$: the residual
  uncertainty about which task an instruction denotes, given only its words of
  class $W$ (nouns or verbs). This is a property of the benchmark rather than of a
  model, and we use it in Section~\ref{sec:info} to establish which perturbations
  can carry evidence about grounding at all.
\end{itemize}

\section{Empirical Results \& Analysis}

\subsection{What the Instruction Actually Encodes}
\label{sec:info}
Perturbation studies infer which word classes a policy grounds by corrupting each
in turn and reading off the damage. This is only valid if the word classes carry
comparable information about the task. On \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} they do not, and
the gap is total rather than marginal.

We parse each of the ten instructions into its unordered noun set and its verb
set, and compute the residual uncertainty about task identity given each. With a
uniform prior over the ten tasks, $H(\text{task}) = \log_2 10 = 3.32$ bits:
\begin{equation}
H(\text{task}\mid\text{noun set}) = 0.00\ \text{bits},\qquad
H(\text{task}\mid\text{verb set}) = 1.55\ \text{bits}.
\end{equation}
All ten noun sets are distinct, so the nouns alone determine the task. The verbs
do not: \texttt{put} is the verb of six of the ten tasks. Conditional on the
nouns, the verb therefore contributes \emph{exactly zero} bits.
Figure~\ref{fig:information} shows both the residual uncertainty and the partition
that produces it.

Two consequences follow, and both undercut the standard reading of these probes.
First, a policy that ignores the verb entirely loses no task-relevant information
on this suite; verb-insensitivity is the optimal policy, not a grounding deficit.
Second, and more damaging for the method, the verb-substitution probe cannot
detect verb-insensitivity even in principle: seven of its ten substitutions
replace \texttt{put} with \texttt{push}, which leaves the noun set---and hence the
task---unchanged. Only three tasks (\texttt{open}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{close}
twice, \texttt{turn on}$\leftrightarrow$\texttt{turn off} once) substitute a true
antonym in a context where the nouns cannot disambiguate the goal. We report those
three separately throughout, and treat them as the only valid verb probes the
suite affords.

We stress that this is a property of the benchmark, not of any model, and it is
computed from the task definitions alone with no rollouts. We suggest that
instruction-level information content be reported as a matter of course before
word-class grounding claims are drawn from perturbation experiments.

\subsection{The Decisive Test: Removal, Not Substitution}
\label{sec:ablation}
The argument above makes a sharp prediction, and it is falsifiable. Instead of
\emph{substituting} within a word class---which, as just shown, mostly fails to
change the task---\emph{remove} a class outright and measure the cost. Dropping the
verb (``the middle drawer of the cabinet'') removes $0$ bits of task information and
should cost close to nothing. Masking the nouns (``open the thing of the thing'')
removes all $3.32$ bits and should be catastrophic. Had verb removal instead
collapsed performance, the redundancy account would be wrong: the information
analysis would be describing the task distribution while missing what the policy
actually consumes.

The prediction holds, and with an unusually clean separation
(Table~\ref{tab:rq_ablation}, Figure~\ref{fig:removal}). Deleting the verb entirely leaves OpenVLA-OFT's success
rate statistically unchanged on the same initial scenes---a point estimate of
$\oftVerbDrop$ points with a $95\%$ interval of $[-5.0, +1.0]$ that contains zero,
and a McNemar $p$ of $0.34$. Of $200$ matched pairs only $10$ changed outcome at
all, $7$ one way and $3$ the other, which is what a coin looks like. Masking the
nouns costs $\oftNounMask$ points, flips $148$ pairs, and never once flips a
failure into a success. Because every perturbed episode starts from the same
simulator state as its baseline, these are matched pairs and we test them as
such; the discordant counts are the evidence, and the concordant majority
correctly drops out. The two edits are comparable in
surface magnitude---each removes a small number of tokens from a short
instruction---and they differ by two orders of magnitude in effect. What separates them
is not how much text was deleted but how much task information it carried.

Two details are worth noting. First, \texttt{nouns\_masked} remains clearly \emph{above}
\texttt{blank}, even though on our accounting both destroy all $3.32$ bits. The masked
instruction retains the verb and the syntactic frame, so the residual gap is a
reasonable estimate of what a policy can extract from instruction \emph{structure}
once reference is gone---which is more than nothing and much less than the nouns
provide. Second, this asymmetry is invisible to the standard probe: substituting the
verb moves OpenVLA-OFT by no detectable amount, even on the three tasks where the
substitution is contrastive at all (Table~\ref{tab:rq2_wordclass}), so a study
running only substitution would conclude that this model ignores verbs, and a study
running only removal would agree---but neither would establish that ignoring them is
the correct policy here. That requires the information analysis, not another rollout.

\subsection{The Scene-Fixed Invariant Is Easy to Break Silently}
\label{sec:scenefixed}
Our own protocol requires that all conditions for a (task, episode) start from an
identical simulator state. Auditing this with the state hash, we found the
invariant violated across most of our initial data, for a reason that we believe
generalises to other LIBERO-based evaluations.

The initial state is \emph{not} a pure function of (task, episode). The
environment is seeded once at construction, and each \texttt{reset} advances that
generator, which perturbs object poses even though the stored initial state pins
\texttt{qpos} and \texttt{qvel}. The scene an episode receives therefore also
depends on how many resets its process has already served. Any condition
completed by a \emph{second} process---after a wall-clock kill, or simply because
conditions were run in separate jobs---silently lands on different scenes, while
the output files look ordered, complete and plausible. In our data the clearest
case is a single condition split across a job boundary: episode~9 sits on a
different scene from episodes~0--8 of the same condition.

This matters beyond bookkeeping. Recomputing our paraphrase deltas against a
scene-matched rather than a pooled baseline moves them by up to $12$ percentage
points---comparable to the effect sizes such studies typically report. We
therefore (i) treat the task as the atomic unit of evaluation, so that every
condition for a task is produced by one process or regenerated wholesale, and
(ii) make the invariant check a hard failure in the aggregation step rather than
a logged diagnostic. Both are released with the code.

collapse success, establishing that the instruction is causally load-bearing.
SmolVLA falls to exactly zero under both ($\text{CSS}=1.00$); OpenVLA-OFT retains a
small residue, $\oftBlank\%$ and $\oftNonsense\%$ against a $\oftBaseline\%$ baseline
($\text{CSS}=\oftCssBlank$ and $\oftCssNonsense$), which we return to in the
Discussion. Nothing here
is a graded contrast, so the conclusion does not rest on the exact residue.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq1_scale} shows this is flat in scale.
The two semantic-swap conditions are the ones that have been over-read, and we take
them in turn.

\paragraph{What the scene-fixed audit lets us claim.} Our own check
(Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed}) is what decides which rows appear at all. A model
whose grid fails the check is left blank rather than printed with a footnote: its
graded contrasts are confounded by differing initial scenes, and we would rather
show a table with a hole in it than a number a reader can quote. This is not
hypothetical. Every number reported here for OpenVLA-7B is from its \emph{second}
grid: the first failed the check on $20$ of $80$ comparable episodes, was withheld
from an earlier draft of this table, and was re-run from scratch. All three grids
now pass at $200$ comparable episodes with zero mismatches, which is the state the
table reports.

The distinction worth drawing is between a floor and a gradient. A scene confound can
inflate or deflate a partial difference, but it cannot manufacture a floor:
\texttt{wrong\_task} sits at exactly $0\%$ for every model and every episode, and no
redistribution of initial scenes turns a uniform zero into a $78$--$98\%$ baseline.
The CSS$\,\approx\!1$ result is therefore the robust part, while the intermediate
rates are the fragile part, and we lean only on the former.

\paragraph{Object swaps measure compliance, not collapse.}
Read naively, \texttt{wrong\_object} triggers a near-total collapse in TSR. But
TSR here scores the policy against the \emph{original} task, so a policy that
correctly follows the altered instruction is recorded as a failure. The condition
also mixes three cases that warrant different treatment: the perturbed string may
name another real task, describe a novel but coherent goal, or be degenerate
(``put the bowl in the bowl'', ``put the plate on the plate''), where failure
carries no information about grounding at all.

The first case is decisive. For one task the object swap yields, verbatim, the
instruction of another task in the suite. Every model we can currently score performs
\emph{that} task and never the original: SmolVLA $17/20$ episodes, OpenVLA $18/20$
and OpenVLA-OFT $20/20$, with $0$ executions of the original task by any model. This is precise
instruction-following, scored as total failure. We therefore report OAR against
the \emph{named} task alongside TSR, and caution that headline ``collapse''
numbers under object substitution should not be read as loss of grounding.

\paragraph{Verb swaps mostly do not test verbs.}
Seven of the ten verb substitutions preserve the noun set and therefore the task
identity (Section~\ref{sec:info}), so surviving them is expected of any competent
policy and says nothing about verb grounding. Splitting the condition into the three
genuine antonym pairs and the seven near-synonyms separates the informative cases
from the rest (Table~\ref{tab:rq2_wordclass}).

changes the task. Only the antonym column tests verb grounding; the near-synonym

The split changes the conclusion, and OpenVLA-7B shows why in the sharpest form.
Read whole, its \texttt{wrong\_action} condition costs $12$ points---a mild effect,
the kind routinely reported as evidence that verbs are near-optional. Split, that
average dissolves into two opposite findings: the three antonym substitutions cost
it $31.7$ points ($p<10^{-5}$, $19$ of $60$ pairs flipped to failure and none the
other way), while the seven near-synonyms cost $3.6$ and are indistinguishable from
no edit at all ($p=0.52$). OpenVLA reads the verb, decisively, and the suite-wide
probe hides it by averaging one real effect against seven non-events.

SmolVLA behaves the same way, less starkly: $21.7$ points on antonyms ($p=0.002$)
against $10.7$ on near-synonyms ($p=0.02$). OpenVLA-OFT is the exception. It loses
$5.0$ points on antonyms and $2.1$ on near-synonyms and \emph{neither} is
distinguishable from zero ($p=0.45$, $p=0.51$); of its $60$ antonym pairs, $5$
flipped to failure and $2$ flipped back. Told to ``turn off the stove'' it turns the
stove on, completing the \emph{original} task in $55$ of $60$ episodes. So the
verb-insensitivity the literature attributes to VLAs in general is, on this suite,
a property of one of the three models we tested---and the probe that is usually
used to establish it could not have detected it in the other two.

One distinction deserves care, because the two 7B models make it visible.
\emph{Removing} the verb is free for both---OpenVLA gains $4.0$ points, OpenVLA-OFT
loses $2.0$, neither detectable (Table~\ref{tab:rq_ablation})---while
\emph{contradicting} it costs OpenVLA $31.7$. Redundant is not the same as ignored.
A word can carry no task information, so that deleting it changes nothing, and still
be read closely enough that asserting its opposite derails the policy. Only
comparing removal against substitution separates those two states, which is the
methodological point of this section.

This split remains the most underpowered comparison in the paper: the suite affords
only three antonym pairs, so $n$ grows with episodes but never with tasks. We also
note a correction it forced. An earlier draft, built when each cell held two
episodes, put OpenVLA-OFT's antonym gap at exactly zero on $n=12$ and called the
model unmoved. At $n=60$ the estimate is $5.0$ points, which looked like a real
weakening until it was tested: the interval $[-13.3, +3.3]$ covers both numbers.
The lesson runs against the point estimates, not for them.

\subsection{Paraphrase Robustness}
\label{sec:rq2}
Corrupting an instruction asks whether language is used at all. Paraphrasing it asks
a harder question: whether the policy has bound to \emph{meaning} or to surface form.
We rewrite each instruction along three axes---the object expression, the action
expression, and both at once---and score the results with PRIDE
\citep{kim2026liberopara}, which weights each paraphrase's success by how far it
departs from the original (Table~\ref{tab:rq2_para}).

The spread across models is large and orders with scale: SmolVLA degrades severely
($4.72\%$ overall TSR, PRIDE $2.7$), OpenVLA sits in between ($44.22\%$, PRIDE
$33.3$), and OpenVLA-OFT holds $74.22\%$ (PRIDE $65.8$). All three
order the axes the same way: object paraphrases hurt least, compositional most, which
is what one expects if the noun phrase is what the policy is keying on. We do not
attribute OpenVLA-OFT's robustness to FiLM: the checkpoint we evaluate is the plain
OFT release (\texttt{use\_film=false} in our config) and no FiLM-trained LIBERO-Goal
checkpoint is public, so that comparison is simply untested.

Two measurement caveats bound how these numbers should be read, both consequences
of Section~\ref{sec:scenefixed}. First, $\Delta$TSR compares a paraphrase pool
against the \texttt{original} pool, and the two were produced by different processes,
so whether they share initial scenes has to be checked rather than assumed. It varies
by model: OpenVLA-OFT's paraphrase pool covers $15$ scenes per task and contains all
$10$ of the scenes in its baseline, so the scene-matched delta is computable on the
whole baseline; SmolVLA's covers only $2$ scenes per task, both of which are in its
baseline, so its delta is scene-matched but rests on a fifth of the available scenes.
Restricting the baseline to the matched subset shifts each delta by roughly ten
percentage points, which is the size of the effects such studies typically report.
Crucially the \emph{ordering} across axes (object $<$ action $<$ compositional) is
identical under either baseline, so we treat the ordering as the result and the
magnitudes as provisional; both ship in the released CSV
(\texttt{delta\_TSR\_pp} and \texttt{delta\_TSR\_pp\_scene\_matched}). Second, episode
counts overstate the independent variation behind these estimates: SmolVLA's $2963$
compositional paraphrase episodes span only those two distinct initial scenes per
task, because the paraphrase runs predate our initial-state cycling fix. We therefore
report the number of distinct scenes alongside $n$, and avoid confidence intervals
computed as though episodes were independent draws.

\section{Discussion}
Three things are worth separating in what we found.

\paragraph{Language is load-bearing, and scale does not change that.} Destroying the
instruction destroys success for all three architectures, across a $16\times$ range
and across discretized, continuous and flow-matching action heads. Nothing suggests
that a bigger backbone learns to do without the instruction. This is the one headline
that came through the re-analysis untouched.

\paragraph{The asymmetry we set out to explain was mostly ours to begin with.} We
began this project intending to give a mechanistic account of why VLAs bind nouns
more tightly than verbs. The account we found is that on this suite there is little
to explain: the verb carries no task information once the nouns are known, so
ignoring it is correct; seven of ten verb probes cannot detect verb-blindness even in
principle; and most of the object-swap ``collapse'' is a policy doing exactly what it
was told, scored against a task it was not asked to do. The numbers themselves are
not in dispute---our TSRs reproduce the pattern others report---only what may be
concluded from them. What survives is narrower: OpenVLA-OFT ignores even contrastive
antonyms. We prefer the narrow claim the design supports to the broad one it does not.

\paragraph{Extra observation streams and residual shortcutting.} OpenVLA-OFT is the
only model here with a wrist camera and proprioception, and also the only one that
retains any measurable success when the instruction is unusable
(Table~\ref{tab:rq1_causal}: $\oftBlank\%$ under \texttt{blank}, against exactly zero
for the single-camera SmolVLA). It is
tempting to read this as richer non-linguistic input enabling a visual shortcut, and
we think that is the most plausible reading---but with one model in that
configuration we cannot separate the observation streams from the action head or the
fine-tuning recipe, and we do not claim to. It is not attributable to FiLM, which
this checkpoint does not use.

\section{Conclusion}
We presented a scene-fixed causal and paraphrase evaluation of three
Vision-Language-Action models spanning $16\times$ in scale. Language is causally
necessary and scale-invariant. The asymmetric noun~$\gg$~verb grounding gap that
such evaluations are usually taken to demonstrate does not, we argue, follow from
them: on \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} the verb is informationally redundant given the
nouns, most verb-substitution probes leave task identity intact, and most
object-substitution ``failures'' are the policy complying with the instruction it
received. The claim that survives is narrower and better supported---on the only
three tasks where a verb swap changes the goal, OpenVLA-OFT shows no detectable
response to it ($p=0.45$) while SmolVLA does ($p=0.002$)---and we prefer it to the
broader one.

The general lesson is that instruction-perturbation studies inherit the information
structure of their benchmark, and that structure has to be measured before the
behavioural results can be read as evidence about grounding.

This has a direct bearing on the mitigations now being proposed. Several recent
methods amplify the language signal at inference or flatten the loss landscape during
finetuning, and report large gains on instruction-following metrics
\citep{zhan2026stable,zhang2026flatness}. Residual Affordance Steering
\citep{zhan2026stable} is, up to normalisation, the contrastive operator
$\pi(a\mid o,l) \propto \exp[s(a\mid o,\emptyset) + \gamma\,\Delta_{\text{sem}}]$ with
$\Delta_{\text{sem}} = s(a\mid o,l) - s(a\mid o,\emptyset)$ that we had ourselves
sketched as future work, and it evidently helps. Our results do not dispute those
gains; they question what the gains are measured against. If a benchmark's verbs are
redundant given its nouns, then a method that increases verb sensitivity is being
scored on a probe that cannot distinguish better grounding from a policy pushed away
from the information-theoretically optimal one. We would want to see such
improvements demonstrated on a set of instructions whose word classes are known to be
contrastive.

That points to what we think the field actually needs: suites built with
verb-contrastive minimal pairs, of which \texttt{LIBERO-Goal} affords exactly three.
Building them is cheap compared with training another policy, and until they exist,
the honest move is to publish the information content of the instruction set
alongside the success rates---as we do here---so that readers can see which
comparisons carry evidence and which are structurally incapable of it.

\section*{Limitations}
Our study is deliberately narrow to keep the causal claim clean, and this bounds
its scope. (i) \textbf{Single suite}: the causal isolation relies on
\texttt{LIBERO-Goal}'s shared initial scene and does not transfer to
scene-confounded suites; we therefore do not claim the asymmetry holds under
visual variation. (ii) \textbf{Simulation only}: all results are in MuJoCo/LIBERO
with no real-robot evaluation. (iii) \textbf{Sample size}: several cells rest on a
modest number of episodes, so we treat the qualitative pattern rather than any point
estimate as the claim, and report $n$ and a confidence interval for every cell so a
reader can see which comparisons are underpowered. The antonym contrast is the
narrowest of them: $60$ episodes per model, but only ever three tasks, so episodes
buy precision and never coverage---which is why we read it as a claim about one model
rather than a trend. We note that going from two episodes per cell to ten moved
OpenVLA-OFT's antonym gap from $0.0$ to $5.0$ points, so we do not treat the
remaining point estimates as settled either. (iv) \textbf{Scope of the information argument}: $H(\text{task}\mid W)$ is
computed over this suite's ten instructions under a uniform task prior. It shows
that verbs are redundant \emph{here}; it does not show that VLAs would ignore
verbs on a suite where verbs are contrastive, and our three antonym pairs are too
few to settle that. The uniform prior is itself an assumption: a policy trained on a
skewed task distribution faces a different effective entropy than the one we compute
from the task definitions.
(v) \textbf{Coverage of the removal test}: the removal conditions were run on both
7B models and replicate ($n=200$ each; verb deletion undetectable at $p=0.36$ and
$p=0.34$, noun masking at $p<10^{-10}$ for both). They were not run on SmolVLA,
whose $\sim\!161$\,s per episode made two extra conditions cost roughly $18$ GPU-hours
we chose to spend on the causal grid instead; that omission is a budget decision, not
a methodological one, and SmolVLA's \texttt{blank} condition is consistent with the
same account. (vi) \textbf{No mechanistic account}: we deliberately make no
attention-level claim. An earlier draft reported a layer-wise noun-vs-verb
cross-attention ratio; we found the underlying extraction had compared one model
against a relabelled copy of another and had fed the OpenVLA-family models a blank
image, so we removed the analysis rather than report it. A corrected measurement is in
progress and is not claimed here. Readers wanting a mechanistic picture should see
\citet{grant2026features}, who use activation injection, sparse autoencoders and
linear probes across six models; our contribution is behavioural and
information-theoretic, and is compatible with their finding that the visual pathway
dominates on suites where the scene identifies the task. (vii) \textbf{Models}: we
evaluate three open checkpoints but a single benchmark family, and the coverage
actually reported is whatever passes the scene-fixed check---see the draft-status
note in Section~\ref{sec:setup}. The $16\times$ scale range spanned by the models we
report on is what carries the scale-invariance claim; it does not depend on the
intermediate checkpoint.

\section*{Ethics and Reproducibility}
This work uses only simulated manipulation tasks and public model checkpoints; it
involves no human subjects or personal data. The primary societal consideration is
safety: a policy that silently ignores the action verb could execute an unintended
manipulation while appearing to follow instructions, which our diagnostics are
designed to expose. For reproducibility we release per-run provenance (model,
checkpoint hash, code revision, seed and wall-clock in a run manifest), pinned
environment locks for each backbone, and the scene-fixed invariance audit. Every
table in this paper is generated from the result files by a script rather than
transcribed by hand; we adopted that rule after finding that hand-copied numbers
were how an error survived several drafts of this work.
